The problem

Why this needed to exist

Electronics are high-consideration purchases, but listings are written for experts. A typical laptop title reads "Core Ultra 5-228V / 14inch WUXGA / 512GB SSD / 32GB RAM / Shared Intel Arc 130V". A parent buying for a university student knows the need, not which of those numbers matter.

Retail sites already offer good category guides and filters. The gap is a guide that starts from the shopper's need, works in Arabic as well as English, and is honest about what each option gives up.

The product

How it works

  1. The shopper writes a need in English or Arabic, for example "لابتوب للجامعة بميزانية 3000 درهم".
  2. Worth It reads the category, budget, use (study, gaming, sunny room, camera) and any brand preference. If the category is missing, it asks one short question instead of guessing.
  3. It ranks only products in the catalogue, so it cannot invent a model or a price.
  4. It returns a best fit, a spend less option and, when it is worth it, a worth stretching option just above budget.
  5. Each card gives plain-language reasons and one honest trade-off, such as an international version with an English-only keyboard, or OLED being less bright in a sunny room.
  6. Shoppers can compare up to three products side by side, and follow-up chips like "something cheaper" keep the context.
Product decisions

The calls that mattered

Always show a cheaper option

Shoppers trust advice that does not only upsell. Showing what you give up by spending less builds that trust, and the guardrail metric checks it does not hurt basket value.

One trade-off per product

A long list of cons is as hard to read as a spec sheet. One honest downside is enough to make a confident choice.

Arabic from day one

Gulf dialect, Arabic digits and Arabic product terms are parsed, and answers come back in the shopper's language. Product names stay in English, as they appear on the box.

Rules first, model second

The live demo runs on a rules engine, so it answers instantly, costs nothing and works for every visitor. I have prototyped a language-model layer, grounded in the same catalogue, for open-ended follow-ups. The rules stay as the fallback when the model is unavailable.

Measuring success

What I'd measure

Time to decisionTime from first question to add-to-cart
